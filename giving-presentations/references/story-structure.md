# Story and Structure

Use this reference to help users turn ideas, data, research, or decisions into a coherent presentation narrative.

## The core model

A presentation should move an audience. Define the movement before writing slides.

| Element | Question | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Who are they and what do they care about? | Named audience and priorities |
| Current state | What do they believe or do now? | “They currently think/do…” |
| Desired state | What should change? | “Afterward they should…” |
| Arrow | What should they remember? | One sentence |
| Stakes | Why does it matter now? | Cost of inaction / upside of action |
| Path | How do we get there? | 3–5 narrative beats |
| Proof | Why should they believe us? | Data, demo, story, authority, precedent |
| Ask | What do we want them to do? | Decision/action/commitment |

## The arrow test

A strong arrow is:

- Specific enough that the opposite could be true.
- Memorable in one sentence.
- Valuable to the audience, not just the presenter.
- Supported by every major section.
- Repeated at the opening, midpoint, and close in varied language.

Weak arrows:

- “I will explain the project.”
- “This is an update on Q3.”
- “We should discuss options.”
- “The audience will understand the data.”

Stronger arrows:

- “We should fund the corrosion imaging pipeline now because it removes the current failure-analysis bottleneck before the next outage cycle.”
- “Our growth constraint has shifted from acquisition to activation, so the next product bet should be onboarding, not top-of-funnel spend.”
- “The result is not just statistically significant; it changes which mechanism we should design around.”

## Audience journey

Use this before choosing a structure.

1. **Before:** What does the audience believe, feel, know, or do now?
2. **Obstacle:** What prevents them from accepting the message?
3. **Need:** What proof, language, story, or confidence do they need?
4. **After:** What should they believe, feel, know, or do after the talk?
5. **Action:** What should happen next?

Phrase it as:

> “This audience currently thinks ______. They are blocked by ______. By the end, they should believe ______ because ______, and they should ______ next.”

## Narrative frameworks

### 1. What is / what could be

Best for: persuasive talks, strategy, change management, keynotes, vision talks, fundraising, product narratives.

Structure:

1. Establish current reality.
2. Show why it is insufficient, costly, or fragile.
3. Glimpse the better future.
4. Return to present constraints.
5. Introduce the insight or solution.
6. Prove the path is credible.
7. Paint the future state.
8. Ask the audience to act.

Use contrast repeatedly. Contrast can be narrative, visual, numeric, emotional, or vocal.

### 2. Scientific/technical bowtie

Best for: scientific talks, conference talks, technical briefings, research updates.

Structure:

1. Start broad: real-world context, scientific field, or engineering problem.
2. Narrow to the specific gap or question.
3. State the hypothesis, objective, or decision-relevant question.
4. Present 2–4 evidence pillars.
5. Dive into methods only when needed to trust the evidence.
6. Surface the result and its interpretation.
7. Broaden back out: implications, limitations, next experiment, decision.

Modulate “data dive” depth by audience. Executives need implications; peers need enough methods to trust the claim; specialists may need assumptions and uncertainty.

### 3. Problem → insight → proof → path

Best for: business cases, product strategy, internal proposals.

1. The problem is larger or different than we thought.
2. The non-obvious insight explains why.
3. The proof shows this is real.
4. The path shows what to do.
5. The ask makes the next step easy.

### 4. Situation → complication → question → answer

Best for: consulting-style presentations, executive updates, crisp decision meetings.

1. Situation: shared context.
2. Complication: what changed, broke, or became urgent.
3. Question: the decision or issue this creates.
4. Answer: recommendation, evidence, and implications.

Use the answer early when the audience is senior or time-poor.

### 5. Demo narrative

Best for: product demos, technical demos, sales demos, tool walkthroughs.

1. Name the user and their painful job.
2. Show the old way briefly.
3. Show the new way with minimal setup.
4. Narrate the user value, not the UI labels.
5. Reveal the technical or business insight behind the magic.
6. Quantify the outcome.
7. Close with what the audience can do next.

Avoid “feature tourism.” A demo should tell the story of a user succeeding.

### 6. Teaching ladder

Best for: lectures, training, workshops, onboarding, technical education.

1. Motivation: why learners should care.
2. Map: what they will be able to do.
3. Prerequisites: concepts they need first.
4. Step 1 with example.
5. Check for understanding or interaction.
6. Step 2 with practice.
7. Common failure modes.
8. Summary and next action.

Use segmenting: teach in chunks and give the audience a chance to process.

### 7. Personal story / keynote arc

Best for: inspirational talks, conference talks, founder stories, career talks.

1. Opening image or moment.
2. Stakes: why this mattered.
3. The old belief.
4. The tension or failure.
5. The turning point.
6. The new belief.
7. How the audience can apply it.
8. Closing image that returns to the opening.

Keep the audience as the hero. Personal stories are useful when they unlock meaning for the listener.

## Openings

A strong opening should orient and create appetite. Use one of these:

- **Cold open:** a vivid story, moment, or surprising result.
- **Decision first:** “Today I’m asking you to approve X.”
- **Tension first:** “We’ve solved Y, but Z is now the bottleneck.”
- **Question first:** “What would have to be true for this to work?”
- **Data with meaning:** one number translated into a relatable image.
- **Promise:** “By the end, you’ll know how to decide between A and B.”

Avoid opening with logistics unless the format requires it.

## Closings

The close should land the plane, not fade out.

A good close usually has:

1. A short callback to the opening.
2. The arrow in memorable language.
3. The decision or action needed.
4. Confidence about the future state.
5. A clean final sentence.

Useful final-line patterns:

- “The choice today is not whether this matters; it is whether we move before the bottleneck becomes irreversible.”
- “If you remember one thing, remember this: ______.”
- “This is the moment where we stop treating ______ as a side project and make it the operating system for ______.”
- “The next step is simple: approve ______, assign ______, and review ______ by ______.”

## Transitions

Transitions carry authority. Do not leave the audience to infer why the next slide exists.

Good transition patterns:

- “That shows the size of the problem. Now let’s look at why it is happening.”
- “If that were the only issue, we could stop here. The harder part is…”
- “So far, this is a risk story. The next slide is the opportunity story.”
- “Before we talk solution, I want to rule out the obvious alternative.”
- “This is where the technical detail matters.”

## Evidence choices

Match evidence to the audience’s decision criteria.

| Audience need | Best evidence |
|---|---|
| “Is this real?” | Customer quotes, repeated observations, independent data |
| “Is this big enough?” | Market size, frequency, financial impact, risk cost |
| “Can we do it?” | Demo, prototype, case study, resource plan |
| “Will it work here?” | Pilot results, comparable rollout, constraints and mitigations |
| “Is the science sound?” | Methods, controls, uncertainty, replication, limitations |
| “What should I decide?” | Options, tradeoffs, recommendation, next step |

## Pacing

For a 10–20 minute talk, use 3–5 major sections. More than that usually becomes a list.

Approximate timing:

- Opening and promise: 10–15%.
- Current reality and tension: 20–25%.
- Core evidence or demo: 35–45%.
- Recommendation or implication: 15–20%.
- Close and ask: 5–10%.

For a senior executive audience, move the recommendation earlier and push detail into backup.

## Story smell tests

Something is wrong if:

- The presentation can be rearranged without changing the meaning.
- The first five minutes do not reveal why the audience should care.
- The conclusion is only visible on the final slide.
- Every slide begins with “overview,” “background,” “results,” or “next steps.”
- The story depends on the presenter reading all text aloud.
- The audience could ask “so what?” after any section.
