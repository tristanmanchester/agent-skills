# Rehearsal and Delivery

Use this reference when the user wants to practice, feel calmer, sound more confident, improve stage/Zoom presence, create speaker notes, manage timing, or prepare for live delivery.

## Rehearsal hierarchy

Rehearse in layers rather than trying to perfect the whole talk immediately.

1. **Story run:** Can you explain the talk without slides?
2. **Structure run:** Can you move through the sections in order?
3. **Transition run:** Can you bridge between sections cleanly?
4. **Timing run:** Can you hit the time with natural pacing?
5. **Delivery run:** Can you add pauses, eye contact, vocal variation, and gesture?
6. **Pressure run:** Can you recover from interruptions, skipped slides, and hard questions?

## Internalize, do not over-memorize

Memorize only the exact lines that need precision:

- Opening.
- Core arrow.
- Sensitive claims.
- Important transitions.
- Closing ask.

For the rest, use talking points and landmarks. Word-for-word memorization can become brittle; if the speaker loses one phrase, they may lose the thread. A resilient speaker knows the map, not just the script.

## Accordion method

Use this to compress the talk to its essence and then expand it again.

1. Deliver the talk in the full allotted time.
2. Deliver it in half the time.
3. Deliver it in 3 minutes.
4. Deliver it in 60 seconds.
5. Deliver it in 30 seconds.
6. Write the one-sentence arrow.
7. Re-expand to 3 minutes.
8. Re-expand to the full time.

This reveals what is essential, what is optional, and which transitions carry the story.

## Rehearsal schedule by time remaining

### If the talk is tomorrow

- Define the arrow and close.
- Rewrite slide titles into claims.
- Practice the opening 5 times.
- Practice transitions once.
- Do one timed run.
- Prepare 10 likely questions.
- Sleep rather than making endless minor slide edits.

### If the talk is in a week

- Day 1: story spine and slide plan.
- Day 2: draft slides and speaker notes.
- Day 3: cut and simplify.
- Day 4: timed run and record.
- Day 5: Q&A drill and backup slides.
- Day 6: realistic run with setup.
- Day 7: light review, vocal warm-up, logistics.

### If the talk is high-stakes

- Run a red-team rehearsal with someone playing the hardest stakeholder.
- Rehearse under realistic constraints: standing, clicker, camera, timer, venue or video setup.
- Prepare alternate paths: short version, no-slides version, Q&A-first version.
- Get pre-alignment before the meeting if the outcome depends on specific people.

## Speaker notes that work live

Use short cues, not paragraphs.

Good note structure:

- **Land:** one-sentence point.
- **Say:** key phrase or number.
- **Show:** where to direct attention.
- **Pause:** where to let it breathe.
- **Ask:** question or interaction.
- **Bridge:** transition to next slide.

Example:

> Land: Activation, not acquisition, is the growth constraint.  
> Say: “We are paying to refill a leaky bucket.”  
> Show: left side of funnel chart.  
> Pause after “leaky bucket.”  
> Bridge: “So what is causing the leak?”

## Managing nerves

Nerves are not a sign the talk will go badly. They are energy. The job is to channel that energy into clarity and connection.

### Before speaking

- Breathe with a longer exhale than inhale, such as 3 counts in and 6 counts out.
- Try a double inhale followed by a slow exhale if physically comfortable.
- Visualize the full sequence: walking up, opening, audience responding, closing.
- Warm up the voice with gentle humming, lip trills, or reading the first minute aloud.
- Stand in a grounded posture; feel feet on the floor.
- Review the arrow, opening, and close rather than rereading the whole deck.
- Reframe “I am nervous” as “I have energy for this.”

### During speaking

- Start slower than feels natural.
- Pause after important lines.
- Look up when thinking; avoid looking down at the floor or phone.
- Find friendly faces but do not speak only to them.
- If you lose your place, return to the slide title or arrow.
- Do not apologize for minor slips.
- Finish sentences with energy; avoid trailing off.

### After mistakes

Use recovery lines:

- “Let me say that more simply.”
- “The important point is…”
- “I’ll come back to that in a moment.”
- “Let me separate two ideas here.”
- “Here’s the practical implication.”

Then continue. Most audiences notice recovery more than the mistake.

## Voice and pacing

### Pacing

- Slow down for definitions, numbers, and decision points.
- Speed can rise slightly for familiar context or narrative momentum.
- Pause before and after the key line.
- Use silence to signal confidence and let ideas land.

### Vocal variety

Vary:

- Volume.
- Pace.
- Pitch.
- Pauses.
- Sentence length.
- Emotional tone.

A flat voice makes even strong material feel low-stakes. A constantly intense voice becomes exhausting. Use contrast.

### Emphasis

Put stress on the word that changes the meaning.

- “We need to decide **today**.”
- “This is a **risk** story, not a cost story.”
- “The bottleneck moved from **measurement** to **interpretation**.”

## Body language

Use physical presence to support the message.

- Stand balanced; avoid rocking.
- Gesture on purpose, especially for contrasts, sequences, and scale.
- Step or shift position at section changes if the space allows.
- Keep hands free if possible.
- Avoid turning to read the screen.
- In remote talks, look into the camera for key lines.

## Remote and hybrid delivery

Remote talks need more deliberate energy and structure.

- Put the camera near eye level.
- Place notes near the camera, not below the screen.
- Look into the camera for opening, key claims, and closing.
- Use shorter sections and more state changes.
- Ask specific questions rather than “Any thoughts?”
- Use chat, polls, reactions, or short pauses to create interaction.
- Repeat or paraphrase audience questions before answering.
- Name transitions explicitly because visual attention is fragmented.

Hybrid talks are hardest because the room and remote audience have different experiences. Assign a remote advocate if possible, repeat questions from the room, and design interactions that include remote participants.

## Timing

Build a timing budget before rehearsing.

Example for a 20-minute talk:

- Opening: 2 min.
- Current reality: 4 min.
- Insight and proof: 7 min.
- Recommendation/demo: 4 min.
- Close: 1 min.
- Buffer: 2 min.

If rehearsal runs long, cut whole sections before shaving seconds everywhere. Preserve the opening, arrow, and close.

## Delivery checklist

Before the talk:

- Opening and close practiced aloud.
- Talk can be delivered without speaker notes if necessary.
- Timing tested.
- Q&A bank prepared.
- Backup slides ready for predictable detail questions.
- Tech checked: clicker, screen, sound, internet, camera, microphone.
- Water available.
- Breathing/grounding routine chosen.

During the talk:

- Start with presence, not filler.
- Look at the audience before speaking.
- Let important lines breathe.
- Use slide titles as anchors.
- Stay in character through the ending.

After the talk:

- Capture questions asked.
- Note where attention dipped.
- Note which lines landed.
- Update the deck and Q&A bank for next time.
