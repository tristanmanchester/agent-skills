# Presentation Workflows

Use this reference when the user asks for help creating, improving, or preparing a presentation and you need a practical workflow.

## Fast intake

Ask only the questions needed to make progress. If the user has already provided enough information, do not interrogate them. Proceed with explicit assumptions.

Minimum useful intake:

1. **Audience:** Who is in the room? What do they already know? What do they care about? What will they resist?
2. **Outcome:** What should they think, feel, decide, approve, buy, fund, remember, or do afterward?
3. **Format:** How long? In person, remote, hybrid, async, or recorded? Slides, demo, speech, or discussion?
4. **Stakes:** Inform, persuade, teach, inspire, align, sell, fundraise, defend, or decide?
5. **Content:** What raw material exists: deck, notes, data, paper, product, demo, recording, transcript, or outline?
6. **Constraints:** Brand/style rules, accessibility needs, audience expertise, language, venue, Q&A time, decision deadline.

## The 10-minute first-pass workflow

Use this when the user wants immediate help and the details are thin.

1. Draft assumptions about audience, time, and goal.
2. Define the arrow in one sentence.
3. Write the audience shift: “They currently believe/do X; afterward they should believe/do Y.”
4. Pick a story structure from `story-structure.md`.
5. Create a 5–9 beat story spine.
6. Convert each beat into a slide title or section heading.
7. Add evidence, visual idea, and speaker note for each slide.
8. Add likely objections and Q&A prompts.
9. Add a rehearsal plan appropriate to time remaining.
10. End by listing the highest-leverage next edits.

## From raw idea to presentation

Use this when the user has an idea but no deck.

1. **Message strategy**
   - One-sentence arrow.
   - Why now?
   - Audience’s current belief and desired belief.
   - Stakes if nothing changes.
   - Call to action.

2. **Narrative skeleton**
   - Opening hook.
   - Current reality.
   - Tension or cost.
   - Insight or non-obvious truth.
   - Proposed path.
   - Evidence/proof.
   - Objections and response.
   - Future state.
   - Close and ask.

3. **Slide plan**
   - Use one row per slide.
   - Columns: Slide, takeaway title, visual, evidence, speaker note, transition, risk.
   - Keep titles as claims, not labels.

4. **Delivery plan**
   - Identify 3–5 moments that must land exactly.
   - Add pauses and emphasis cues.
   - Add state changes every 3–5 minutes.
   - Prepare answers to likely questions.

## Deck critique workflow

When reviewing an existing deck, diagnose at three levels.

### Level 1: Strategy

- Is the audience specific?
- Is the desired decision/action clear?
- Is there one memorable arrow?
- Does the deck answer “why now?”
- Does the deck address the audience’s likely objections?

### Level 2: Story

- Do the slide titles create a logical argument if read alone?
- Is there contrast between current state and future state?
- Is the pacing appropriate for the time available?
- Are technical details sequenced from intuition to depth?
- Are transitions clear, or does the deck feel like a stack of unrelated slides?

### Level 3: Slides

- One main idea per slide?
- Is the most important visual element obvious within three seconds?
- Is every chart titled with its conclusion?
- Are text, contrast, reading order, and alt text accessible?
- Are backup details separated from core narrative?

Output format for a critique:

| Priority | Issue | Why it matters | Fix | Example rewrite |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Then give a slide-by-slide table for the highest-impact edits.

## Slide-by-slide planning template

Use this format when producing a deck outline.

| # | Takeaway title | Visual idea | Evidence/source | Speaker note | Transition |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Claim, not label | What the slide should show | Data, story, demo, quote | What to say beyond the slide | Link to next slide |

Good title patterns:

- “X is now the bottleneck, not Y.”
- “Customers are already hacking around this problem.”
- “The risk is small; the cost of waiting is large.”
- “Our result holds across three independent measurements.”
- “The decision today is whether to scale, pause, or change scope.”

## Speaker-notes workflow

Good speaker notes are not a transcript. They are a navigation system.

For each slide, write:

- **Point:** the single sentence the slide must land.
- **Set-up:** one line that orients the audience.
- **Evidence:** the example, number, or observation that proves it.
- **Meaning:** why this matters to the audience.
- **Transition:** the handoff to the next slide.
- **Delivery cue:** pause, gesture, look up, slow down, or invite a response.

Only script exact wording for:

- Opening 30–60 seconds.
- Key phrases that must be said precisely.
- Sensitive claims.
- Transitions between major sections.
- Closing ask.

## Rehearsal workflow

Use when the user has a talk and wants to be ready.

1. Run the talk aloud once for content.
2. Cut or move anything that does not support the arrow.
3. Run again against time; note where pacing breaks.
4. Practice the opening, transitions, and close separately.
5. Do one compressed version at half the allotted time.
6. Do one no-slides version to verify the story is internalized.
7. Record and review for pace, clarity, filler, and energy.
8. Practice likely Q&A using the bank in `qa-stakeholders.md`.
9. Do the final run with the same setup as the real event.

## High-stakes alignment workflow

Use for board meetings, executive reviews, fundraising, partner pitches, or politically sensitive presentations.

1. Identify decision makers, influencers, blockers, and silent veto holders.
2. Write each stakeholder’s likely concern in their own language.
3. Hold pre-meetings or send previews to surface objections early.
4. Role-play hard questions from each stakeholder.
5. Prepare backup slides/data for predictable objections.
6. In the live meeting, open with the decision or outcome needed.
7. Use the deck to facilitate the decision, not to perform every detail.
8. Close by restating the decision, owners, timeline, and unresolved issues.

## When the user is anxious

Do not only reassure. Give them a plan.

- Reduce uncertainty: clarify opening, structure, close, and Q&A.
- Reduce cognitive load: use talking points instead of dense notes.
- Rehearse transitions and recovery lines.
- Add a breathing and grounding routine.
- Reframe symptoms as useful energy.
- Practice in progressively more realistic conditions.

## Output shortcuts

When time is short, offer one of these compact deliverables:

- “Here is the one-sentence arrow and a 7-slide outline.”
- “Here are the top 5 fixes I would make before tomorrow.”
- “Here is a 20-minute rehearsal plan for tonight.”
- “Here are 12 likely questions and the best concise answers.”
- “Here are stronger slide titles for your current outline.”
